# Motivation and habits

## Scope

Explain behavior patterns, repeated actions, cues, and the limits of habit research.

## Prerequisites and distinctions

Identify the action and context. Separate intention, repetition, self-reported automaticity, and observed behavior.

## Teaching sequence

Describe the behavior, the proposed cue, and what would count as a change. Keep population findings distinct from individual predictions.

## Common misconceptions

A habit does not form on one universal timetable. Repetition and motivation are related to behavior but are not interchangeable measures.

## Example 1

Lally and colleagues studied repeated behaviors in daily contexts and modeled changes in self-reported automaticity. The time course varied across participants, so the study does not justify a fixed deadline for every new habit.

## Example 2

A developer might use opening a project as a cue to read the current failing test before editing. That is a proposed routine to try and observe, not evidence that the routine will become automatic after a specified number of days.

## Analogies and limits

Use the cue and action directly. A mechanical switch analogy would overstate how reliably a person responds to a cue.
