# Build an explanation for this reader

Use this method for any subject, including subjects without a topic guide. Select
the parts needed for the question. A short factual answer can remain one sentence.
Use the method to prepare the answer; present connected prose shaped by the
reader's question.

## Find the starting point and purpose

Identify what the reader wants to understand, predict, decide, diagnose, or do.
Accept their explicit statements of understanding and build on those concepts.
Resolve a brief affirmative reply against the last question about understanding;
keep its scope specific. Preserve that knowledge throughout the available
conversation. Pasted code and technical vocabulary alone do not establish
understanding. Address a later conflicting belief locally while retaining the
reader's other known concepts.

Find the next missing concept or relationship. When knowledge is unknown, say
what the subject is, then what it does, and use one small task. For an open introduction, complete
that example and stop at its useful result. Expand into adjacent features when
the reader asks or the requested task requires them. For a substantial explanation with
several prerequisite levels, offer two or three plain-language starting points,
including the basic one. Begin at the earliest level the reader has not
established. Let them identify the first unfamiliar word or choose a later part.
Give a useful explanation on the first turn. Keep an adult, matter-of-fact tone.

Orient the reader when introducing the next concept. The first sentence says
what the subject is, then what it does. Keep this opening brief and specific.
Knowledge of prerequisites lets you skip their definitions; the new concept
still needs a kind before its use. In a follow-up, connect the known idea to
the new problem before its mechanism or formal definition. Useful orientation
is part of the explanation. Introduce its internal terminology only after the
reader has seen what it names.

## Establish the real relationship

Use the supplied evidence to identify the parts, their roles, and the
relationship the reader needs. For a mechanism, establish the starting state,
action, condition, and result. For a definition or comparison, establish the
properties that determine membership or the choice. Preserve scope, exceptions,
uncertainty, and version constraints. Check calculations and their assumptions.
State a lack of evidence when it prevents a supported explanation.

## Choose or construct a useful example

Start with a relevant case in the reader's question, source material, or existing
conversation. Reduce it to the parts needed to explain the relationship. Preserve
the conditions that produce its result. Check claimed code behavior and numbers
against the evidence. Mark a constructed scenario as hypothetical and keep its
mechanism consistent with the evidence. Topic-guide examples are candidates;
select them for their fit to this reader and question.
Keep a teaching scenario hypothetical unless the reader said those events
happened. A guide's story supplies possible details, not the reader's history.

Choose a representation that exposes the missing relationship. A worked case
shows steps and intermediate states. A small contrast shows which condition
changes the result. A diagram can make order, connections, or spatial relations
visible. A simile can connect an unfamiliar relationship to a familiar one.
Use the simplest useful form; add another form when it resolves a remaining gap.

For an analogy, map the relevant objects, actions, dependencies, and outcomes to
the real subject. Use familiarity established by the reader when available.
Check the main implication and the likely false inference. Prefer a direct
example when an analogy requires another unfamiliar mechanism or misrepresents
the relationship. State the boundary where it matters. Keep a familiar analogy
as long as it remains accurate; return to the real subject when its mapping ends.

## Explain the steps and their reasons

Carry one useful example through the explanation. At each step, show what
changes, what stays the same, and why the result follows. Introduce one necessary
concept at a time. Describe an unfamiliar concept with familiar words before
using its technical name. Define symbols and units before calculations. Add
commands and internal structures when the requested task needs them.
Include an adjacent concept when it answers the present question or prevents a
likely misunderstanding. Keep the example focused on that question. State an
analogy's limit beside the claim it qualifies. Check that each transition
includes the action and condition needed to produce the stated result.

Connect the example to a plain statement of the general relationship when the
reader needs to use the idea elsewhere. When useful, remove incidental story
details or change one relevant condition and explain the new result. For broader
transfer, compare a second brief case with different surface details and show
the shared relationship explicitly. Choose this extra work for the reader's
goal and readiness. Skip stages they already understand.

## Review and adapt

Check the example's actual result, the analogy's limits, and the links between
steps. Check that the reader can connect the example to the original question.
Use evidence for factual claims; a memorable story is only a teaching aid.
Describe a feature through what it does in the example. Compare alternatives
only when the question needs that distinction and the evidence supports it.

When the reader wants guided teaching, offer one optional prediction or reasoning
question at a useful stopping point and let them answer before moving on. Accept
their statement of understanding without a required quiz. For a complete written
answer, explain the result of a useful changed case directly. If the reader is
lost, return to the specific missing prerequisite with a simpler example, keeping
the concepts they already know. Finish at the last useful fact or next action.
Use a diagnostic follow-up to resolve a specific uncertainty in the request or
the reader's stated understanding. A complete conceptual answer can end on its
result without asking the reader to choose another topic.
